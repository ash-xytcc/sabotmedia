THE ONE-MINUTE VERSION

The public record gets us almost all the way there.

In September 2025, the White House ordered a broader counterterrorism campaign aimed not only at alleged perpetrators but at networks and support structures. In October, Andy Ngo proposed a foreign-terrorist designation strategy at the White House. Days later, reporting traced a Portland NoBlogs site to A/I; a counterterrorism analyst publicly described foreign designations as the legal route to infrastructure providers, then carried the A/I narrative into Senate testimony. State subsequently created several foreign-terrorism predicates. In August 2026, OFAC designated A/I under a support-and-services provision. What the public record still does not show is the internal referral that turned A/I from infrastructure mentioned in reporting into a formal sanctions target.

01

THE POLICY ENVIRONMENT CHANGES

The target expands from alleged attackers to networks and infrastructure.

The earliest defensible starting point is not A/I itself. It is the administration’s September 2025 decision to treat antifascist and left-wing political violence as an organized counterterrorism problem whose surrounding networks, funders and support structures should be investigated alongside individual actors.

On September 22, President Donald Trump issued an order purporting to designate Antifa as a domestic terrorist organization. Three days later, National Security Presidential Memorandum 7 directed State, Treasury, Justice and Homeland Security to pursue a broader strategy. The White House described a focus extending through recruitment, funding mechanisms, nongovernmental organizations, financial activity and the acts of violence themselves.

This matters because A/I would eventually be targeted as infrastructure. By late September 2025, the administration had already written infrastructure and support systems into its theory of the problem.

02

OCTOBER 8, 2025

Andy Ngo puts the foreign-terrorist designation mechanism on the White House table.

The October 8 White House Antifa roundtable is a primary-source milestone because the relevant proposal is not inferred from a tweet or later commentary. It appears in the official presidential transcript.

“...the State Department should designate antifa—its international arm as a foreign terrorist organization, FTO.”

Andy Ngo, White House roundtable transcript, Oct. 8, 2025

Later in the same event, the international-designation idea was raised again. Trump endorsed moving forward and said Marco Rubio would handle it. The transcript does not show Ngo naming A/I in the room. What it proves is narrower: a foreign-designation strategy was proposed directly to the president and cabinet before European groups were designated and before A/I was named in congressional testimony.

03

OCTOBER 9–16, 2025

Portland becomes the bridge from “Antifa” rhetoric to A/I’s infrastructure.

Days after the White House roundtable, Rose City Counter-Info, hosted through NoBlogs, drew national attention over a post encouraging people to point lasers at federal or police aircraft. Andy Ngo amplified the post. Fox News covered it, and a DHS spokesperson responded in explicitly network-oriented language, promising enforcement against what the department described as Antifa networks.

The technical breadcrumb mattered more than the spectacle. Once attention shifted from the anonymous Portland publication to the infrastructure behind it, the trail led through NoBlogs to Autistici/Inventati.

On October 16, Hudson Crozier of the Daily Caller News Foundation published a story explicitly centered on the foreign technology provider. The article connected Rose City Counter-Info and other publications to A/I’s hosting and privacy infrastructure. It also asked the legal question that would become central: how could the government reach a service provider whose underlying services were otherwise lawful?

04

THE LEGAL ROADMAP

“Job one.” The theory later used against infrastructure providers is stated publicly.

Crozier asked Kyle Shideler of the Center for Security Policy what a foreign-terrorist designation would change. Shideler’s answer is one of the most consequential pieces of the public chronology.

“...going after organizations like this that provide material support would be job one.”

Kyle Shideler to the Daily Caller News Foundation, Oct. 16, 2025

Shideler also identified the legal obstacle without a designation: authorities would struggle because, as he put it, the services they provide are otherwise lawful.

That is not proof that the government adopted Shideler’s advice. It is proof that the legal architecture was publicly described months before A/I was designated: create a terrorist predicate, then use a material-support theory to reach an infrastructure provider.

05

OCTOBER 28, 2025

The media theory enters congressional counterterrorism testimony.

Twelve days after Crozier’s article, Shideler testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee’s Subcommittee on the Constitution. His written testimony described smaller direct-action groups as connected through aligned organizations, online platforms and support networks, and said this support was primarily provided as services rather than financial transactions.

The testimony then named NoBlogs and Autistici/Inventati directly. Its footnote for that claim cites Crozier’s October 16 article.

This gives us an unusually clean transmission chain: a Portland controversy led to reporting about the infrastructure provider; the reporting articulated a material-support route; and less than two weeks later the same A/I narrative entered an official Senate terrorism hearing with the article attached as a source.

06

NOVEMBER 13, 2025

State creates the foreign-terrorism predicates.

The State Department then designated four European organizations: Antifa Ost, Informal Anarchist Federation/International Revolutionary Front, Armed Proletarian Justice, and Revolutionary Class Self-Defense. The designations changed the legal landscape described three weeks earlier.

The later A/I case makes this sequence more important. Treasury’s August 2026 announcement said its action was building on the November 13 State Department action, while the public government narrative against A/I referenced several of the newly designated groups.

That does not establish that those November designations were chosen in order to reach A/I. It does establish that the predicates were created first and later formed part of the government’s case against the infrastructure provider.

07

2026

The framework becomes an international counterterrorism program.

The gap between the November designations and the August A/I action is not empty. In 2026, the administration increasingly framed left-wing and antifascist militancy as a transnational counterterrorism problem. A May counterterrorism strategy emphasized networks and international connections. In July, Secretary of State Marco Rubio convened officials from dozens of countries for a ministerial on political terrorism, while Treasury emphasized financial disruption and the infrastructure sustaining targeted networks.

By the time A/I was designated, “infrastructure” was no longer incidental language. It was an explicit object of the administration’s counterterrorism policy.

08

AUGUST 26, 2026 · Y

OFAC uses the support-and-services hook against A/I.

The legally operative action came from Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control. A/I was added to the Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist under Executive Order 13224.

The public finding is important for what it says and what it does not say. OFAC did not publicly identify A/I as the perpetrator of a specific attack. The Federal Register notice rests on a support-and-services provision, alleging material, financial or technological support, goods or services in support of an act of terrorism.

This is the legal hinge of the entire story. The privacy, hosting and communications infrastructure for which A/I was known became the subject of a material-support theory once the government connected it to designated actors and alleged violent activity.

09

AUGUST 26–28+ · Z

There was no single shutdown order. There was a compliance cascade.

OFAC simultaneously issued General License 36, temporarily authorizing transactions ordinarily necessary to wind down dealings with A/I through September 25. The license was not itself an order telling every provider to terminate service. The sanctions changed the risk calculation for U.S. persons and U.S.-linked infrastructure.

Within days, the consequences became visible through separate chokepoints. The autistici.org domain was placed on serverHold at the registry level; A/I reported payment disruption; and its banking relationship came under sanctions pressure. The servers did not need to be physically seized for services to become difficult or impossible for ordinary users to reach.

That distinction matters. “A/I was shut down” is an understandable shorthand. The more precise description is a cascading sanctions effect transmitted through registry, payment and financial systems.

THE LAST UNRESOLVED HANDOFF

Who opened the A/I file?

At this point the public chronology becomes unusually specific, and then abruptly stops.

We know the administration adopted a network-and-infrastructure strategy. We know a foreign-designation mechanism was proposed at the White House. We know A/I was identified in reporting and then in Senate testimony. We know foreign predicates were created. We know the 2026 policy increasingly emphasized transnational infrastructure. And we know OFAC ultimately designated A/I under a support-and-services provision.

What we do not yet have is the first internal record that names A/I as a proposed sanctions target.

That missing record would answer the most important causal question in this investigation: whether the designation emerged from an existing law-enforcement investigation, a State or Treasury targeting review, a referral from DHS or FBI, outside reporting or advocacy, White House tasking, or some combination of those channels.

WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS NOW

The designation is less mysterious than it first appeared. The decisive referral is not.

Viewed only from August 26, the designation of a small Italian privacy and hosting collective under one of the United States’ strongest counterterrorism authorities looks startlingly specific. Reconstruct the preceding eleven months and the broad logic becomes visible.

The administration widened its counterterrorism framework from alleged perpetrators to networks and enabling structures. A foreign-terrorist designation strategy was proposed in the White House. A Portland NoBlogs controversy became a national security story. Reporting followed the infrastructure trail to A/I and published a legal theory for reaching service providers. That reporting was then cited in Senate testimony that explicitly characterized online support networks as providers of material support through services. State created foreign-terrorism predicates. In 2026, the administration internationalized the framework and explicitly targeted the infrastructure sustaining those networks. OFAC then used a support-and-services provision against A/I.

None of that proves a secret command by Andy Ngo, Hudson Crozier, Kyle Shideler or any other outside figure. It also does not require one. The documented story is already consequential: ideas and allegations moved through public reporting, congressional testimony and official policy while the legal machinery required to reach an infrastructure provider was assembled around them.

The remaining question is narrower and more answerable than where this investigation began. We are no longer asking why the government might have targeted A/I. We are asking who first converted that theory into an actual government targeting file.

That answer should exist somewhere in the administrative record. Until it is public, this investigation remains open.
